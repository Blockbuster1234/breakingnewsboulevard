LONDON — A BBC investigation using hidden cameras has found that several convenience stores on Britain’s high streets are openly selling cocaine, cannabis and prescription‑only medicines without a doctor’s authorization, prompting police raids and calls for tighter regulation of retail outlets. The undercover footage, filmed over three months in cities including London, Manchester and Birmingham, shows shop assistants handing over small packets of white powder labeled “coke” and green‑wrapped bundles resembling cannabis to customers who ask for them discreetly. In other clips, staff retrieve blister packs of strong opioids such as oxycodone and benzodiazepines from behind the counter and place them in ordinary shopping bags after a brief exchange of cash. BBC reporters, posing as ordinary shoppers, said they were able to purchase the substances with little questioning. In one exchange captured on camera, a store employee in East London replied, “Yeah, we’ve got that stuff. Just don’t tell the cops,” before sliding a small bag of white powder across the counter for £20. The investigation follows a rise in drug‑related offences reported by the Home Office, which recorded a 12% increase in possession of Class A substances in England and Wales during 2025. Health officials have warned that the unregulated sale of prescription medicines poses a significant risk of overdose and addiction, particularly when users are unaware of dosage or potential interactions. Metropolitan Police Assistant Commissioner Neil Basu said the footage provides “clear evidence of criminal activity taking place in premises that are supposed to sell everyday goods.” He added that officers have executed warrants at nine stores identified in the BBC report, seizing approximately 1.5 kilograms of suspected cocaine, 800 grams of cannabis resin and dozens of prescription tablets. Four store managers have been arrested on suspicion of supplying controlled substances, and investigations are ongoing into possible links to wider supply chains. The British Retail Consortium condemned the allegations, stating that “any retailer found to be breaking the law will face the full force of the law and will be removed from our membership.” The organization urged members to review staff training and implement stricter verification procedures for age‑restricted and controlled products. Public health experts expressed concern that the easy availability of drugs in neighbourhood shops could undermine efforts to reduce substance misuse. Dr. Leila Hassan, a consultant in addiction medicine at King’s College London, said, “When drugs are sold alongside milk and bread, the perception of risk diminishes, especially among young people who may experiment without understanding the dangers.” She called for increased funding for community outreach and for local councils to consider licensing requirements similar to those for alcohol and tobacco. Home Office Minister Priti Patel responded to the BBC’s findings by announcing a review of current legislation governing the sale of over‑the‑counter medicines and the licensing of convenience stores. “We will not tolerate the use of legitimate businesses as fronts for illegal drug trade,” Patel said in a statement to Parliament. “Any gaps in the regulatory framework will be closed, and enforcement agencies will be given the tools they need to protect the public.” The BBC said it will hand over all raw footage to the police and the Independent Office for Police Conduct for further examination. The broadcaster emphasized that its investigation was conducted in accordance with editorial guidelines and that the identities of the shop assistants have been blurred to protect their privacy while maintaining the integrity of the evidence. As the story develops, local authorities are urging anyone with information about illicit sales in retail premises to come forward anonymously through CrimeStoppers. The outcome of the ongoing police inquiries could lead to new restrictions on what products high‑street mini‑marts are permitted to stock and how they must monitor sales to prevent the diversion of controlled substances.
