Artemis II Leaves Earth Orbit: Crew Captures 'Hello, World' Photo on Way to Moon

Breaking News Boulevard | April 3, 2026 | Space

For the first time in more than five decades, humans have left Earth orbit. The four astronauts aboard NASA's Artemis II mission executed a flawless trans-lunar injection burn on Friday, setting the Orion capsule on a looping trajectory that will carry them around the far side of the Moon and back to Earth — a journey that will take them farther from home than any humans have ever traveled.

The Push Toward the Moon

The critical engine burn — known as trans-lunar injection, or TLI — lasted five minutes and 50 seconds, during which the Orion service module's main engine fired in a long, steady push that added thousands of kilometres per hour to the spacecraft's speed. Mission control in Houston confirmed the burn went "flawlessly," and within minutes, Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen reported that the crew was "feeling pretty good up here on our way to the Moon."

Hansen, the first non-American to travel to the Moon, delivered a message that resonated with millions watching the livestream: "Humanity has once again shown what we are capable of. It's your hopes for the future that carry us now on this journey around the Moon."

'Hello, World'

Within hours of the burn, the crew's first order of business wasn't systems checklists or data logging — it was looking out the window. Commander Reid Wiseman, who previously described the view from Orion as "spectacular," captured the mission's signature photograph: an image titled Hello, World that shows the vast blue Atlantic Ocean framed by Earth's thin atmospheric glow, with green auroras visible at both poles and the planet Venus shining brightly to the lower right.

The perspective is dizzying. Earth appears upside down from the capsule's angle, with the western Sahara and Iberian Peninsula visible on the left and the eastern portion of South America on the right. A second photograph, Artemis II Looking Back at Earth, captures the terminator line — the boundary between day and night — cutting sharply across the globe.

Wiseman initially struggled with camera exposure settings at such extreme distance, comparing the difficulty to "walking out back at your house, trying to take a picture of the moon." But those teething problems are long past. After the TLI burn, Hansen told mission control the crew was "glued to the windows" photographing everything they could see.

The enthusiasm came with an amusing side effect: Wiseman later radioed Houston to ask how to clean the windows, which had been fogged by four astronauts pressing their faces against them.

A Record-Breaking Journey

The Artemis II trajectory will carry the crew more than 4,700 miles (7,600 km) beyond the Moon — edging past the distance record set by Apollo 13 in 1970, when that mission's crew swung even farther out after an onboard explosion forced an emergency course correction. Depending on the fine details of timing and trajectory, Artemis II could become the most distant humans from Earth to date.

The spacecraft is now on a free-return trajectory — a figure-eight path that uses the Moon's gravity to sling the capsule back toward Earth without requiring additional engine burns. The crew will pass the far side of the Moon around April 6 and splash down in the Pacific Ocean around April 10, concluding a roughly 10-day mission.

A Front-Row Solar Eclipse

One of the trip's most anticipated sights is a total solar eclipse — but not the kind most people picture. On approximately the sixth day of the mission, as Orion cruises beyond the Moon, the Moon itself will slide directly in front of the Sun from the crew's perspective. This will reveal the Sun's shimmering corona while Earth hangs visibly off to one side — a three-body alignment that no eclipse watcher on the ground has ever seen in quite that configuration.

Safety Nets Built In

While the mission has so far progressed without a single critical issue, NASA has built in multiple layers of redundancy. Even after the TLI burn, controllers retain the ability to execute an emergency "handbrake turn" — an engine burn that would reverse course and bring the crew back to Earth rapidly. Within the first 36 hours after TLI, the handbrake turn is the fastest option; after that, staying on course around the Moon and falling back naturally becomes equally quick and often simpler.

Orion programme manager Howard Hu told reporters that the team has "run hundreds of thousands of simulations to ensure we are able to get the crew home safely."

What Comes After Splashdown

Artemis II doesn't land on the Moon — that's Artemis III's job, now targeting 2027. But every data point from this flight feeds directly into that landing mission. Life support performance, radiation exposure, navigation accuracy, and manual flight handling are all being logged in real time and streamed to engineers on the ground.

The data will be critical for NASA's long-term goal of establishing a sustained lunar presence, including surface rovers, robotic infrastructure, and a crewed landing at the Moon's south pole — all stepping stones to an eventual Mars mission.

For now, four astronauts are heading further into the void than anyone has gone since 1972. Earth is shrinking behind them. The Moon is growing ahead of them. And history is happening in real time.
