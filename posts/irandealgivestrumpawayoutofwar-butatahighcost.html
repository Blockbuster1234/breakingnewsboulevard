Iran deal gives Trump a way out of war – but at a high cost The prospect of a renewed agreement over Iran’s nuclear programme has emerged as a possible off‑ramp for the United States from the brink of another Middle‑East conflict, yet analysts warn that any such arrangement would come at a steep political and economic price for former President Donald Trump. Since the United States unilaterally withdrew from the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) in May 2018, re‑imposing sweeping sanctions on Tehran, tensions between Washington and Iran have fluctuated between uneasy diplomacy and outright hostility. The JCPOA, negotiated in 2015 by the P5+1 group – the United States, United Kingdom, France, Russia, China and Germany – placed strict limits on Iran’s uranium enrichment in exchange for the lifting of nuclear‑related sanctions. Trump’s decision to abandon the deal was framed as a response to what he called Iran’s “malign behaviour” across the region, but it also eliminated the primary mechanism that had kept Iran’s nuclear ambitions in check. In the intervening years, Iran has steadily expanded its enrichment activities, moving closer to weapons‑grade uranium levels, while the United States has maintained a maximum‑pressure campaign that has crippled Iran’s oil exports and contributed to a deepening economic crisis. The standoff has repeatedly raised fears of a military confrontation, most notably after the January 2020 killing of Iranian General Qasem Soleimani by a US drone strike, which prompted Iranian missile attacks on US bases in Iraq. Recent diplomatic overtures, facilitated by European intermediaries and indirectly supported by the Biden administration, suggest that a limited, step‑by‑step return to JCPOA‑style constraints could be on the table. Under such a framework, Iran would agree to roll back its most advanced enrichment in exchange for phased sanctions relief, while the United States would retain the ability to snap back penalties if Tehran violated the deal. For Trump, who remains a dominant figure in the Republican Party and is positioning himself for a potential 2024 presidential run, endorsing or even tacitly accepting such an arrangement could provide a politically palatable exit from the spectre of war without appearing to concede to Iran. However, the cost of that exit could be considerable. Domestically, Trump’s base has consistently viewed any compromise with Iran as a betrayal of his “America First” rhetoric and a sign of weakness. Re‑engaging with the JCPOA, even in a modified form, would likely trigger fierce criticism from conservative commentators and lawmakers who argue that any concessions embolden Tehran’s regional proxies, including Hezbollah and the Houthis. Internationally, European allies, who have invested significant diplomatic capital in preserving the original deal, would expect the United States to honour its commitments; a half‑hearted or conditional re‑engagement could undermine trust and complicate future negotiations on other issues, such as missile programmes and human rights.
